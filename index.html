Common Questions

This calculator assumes that if you rent instead of buy, you put the money you save each month into savings or investments. For example, if buying would cost $2,000/month and renting costs $1,500/month, it assumes you'd invest that $500 difference. At the end of your chosen timeframe, it compares what you'd have spent and built in each scenario — home equity on the buying side, savings growth on the renting side — so you can see which option actually leaves you in a better financial position. If you wouldn't save the full difference, or already have savings you'd invest instead of a down payment, you can adjust those inputs in the Renting & Saving section.

An excellent guideline is that your total monthly housing costs — including mortgage, taxes, insurance, and HOA — should not exceed 30% of your monthly take-home pay. However, this is a starting point, not a rule. The right answer depends on your other expenses, savings goals, job stability, and local cost of living. A more personalized approach is to add your projected housing costs to your current lifestyle spending and calculate the income needed to comfortably cover both.

The minimum down payment depends on your loan type: conventional loans can go as low as 3%, FHA loans require 3.5%, and VA and USDA loans may require no down payment for eligible buyers. However, putting down less than 20% typically triggers PMI, which adds $60–$200 or more per month depending on the loan size. A 20% down payment eliminates PMI and results in a lower monthly payment, but it requires significantly more upfront cash.

And the down payment is just one piece of what you'll need at closing — you'll also need to budget for purchase closing costs (typically 2–5% of the loan amount), moving and furnishing expenses, and two months of mortgage payments held in reserve. This calculator accounts for all four buckets in the Total Upfront Cost row of your results.

Beyond the mortgage payment, homeowners typically pay property taxes (often 1–3% of home value per year), homeowner's insurance, HOA fees, private mortgage insurance (PMI) if the down payment is under 20%, and 1–2% of the home's value annually in maintenance and repairs. Selling a home also costs roughly 6–8% of the sale price in agent commissions and closing costs. These costs are frequently underestimated and can add hundreds of dollars per month to the true cost of owning a home.

PMI stands for Private Mortgage Insurance. It's a monthly fee lenders require when your down payment is less than 20% of the home's purchase price. PMI protects the lender — not you — in case you default on the loan. Depending on your loan size and down payment, PMI typically costs $60–$200 per month. It's not permanent: once you've built 20% equity in your home (through payments and/or appreciation), you can request cancellation. It's automatically removed by federal law when your equity reaches 22%.

A mortgage payment in its most basic form consists of two components: Principal (the portion that reduces your loan balance) and Interest (the lender's fee for borrowing). Many lenders also collect property taxes and homeowner's insurance monthly through an escrow account — bundling all four into one payment, sometimes called PITI. HOA fees, while not part of the mortgage itself, are an additional fixed monthly cost for homes in managed communities. This calculator displays principal and interest as your mortgage payment, with taxes, insurance, HOA, and other costs broken out separately so you can see the full picture.

Closing costs are fees paid at the time of purchase, separate from your down payment. They typically include loan origination fees, title insurance, appraisal fees, attorney fees, prepaid interest, and government recording fees. For buyers, closing costs generally run 2–5% of the loan amount. On a $300,000 home with 20% down, that's roughly $4,800–$12,000 in addition to your $60,000 down payment. Your lender is required to provide a Loan Estimate within three business days of your application so you can see the specific numbers before committing.

Renting is often the smarter financial choice when home prices are high relative to rents (a price-to-rent ratio above 15), when you plan to move within 3–5 years, when buying would stretch your budget uncomfortably thin, or when the money you'd put toward a down payment could earn a better return invested elsewhere. Renting also offers flexibility that homeownership doesn't — no maintenance costs, no repair surprises, and the ability to relocate without selling. If you rent and consistently invest the difference in cost compared to owning, you may come out ahead financially compared to buying.

Yes — if your monthly rent is lower than what it would cost to own the equivalent home, consistently investing that difference can build significant wealth over time. Putting the savings into a high-yield savings account, money market account, or index fund means you're still building your financial future even without owning property. Depending on market conditions and your investment returns, you may accumulate more in savings than a homeowner builds in equity over the same period. The key word is "consistently" — this strategy only works if the savings habit is maintained.

Compared to other long-term investments, a primary residence is actually a pretty poor performer. Home values historically grow at roughly the rate of inflation — meaning your home isn't really gaining purchasing power over time, it's mostly keeping pace. That said, homeownership does build equity — a form of forced savings that many people wouldn't otherwise maintain — and that's genuinely valuable. And it's not worth locking yourself into a mortgage you can't afford just to open what is effectively a savings account. The honest framing is: a primary home is a savings vehicle with some lifestyle benefits, not an investment. Go in with that expectation and you won't be disappointed.

The price-to-rent ratio (PTR) compares the cost of buying a home to the cost of renting a comparable one. It's calculated by dividing the home's purchase price by the annual rent for a similar property. A PTR below 10 strongly favors buying. Between 10–15, buying may be worth considering. Between 15–20, renting is generally smarter. Above 20, renting is almost always the better financial choice. The PTR is a useful quick filter, but it doesn't replace a full cost comparison that accounts for your specific mortgage terms, tax situation, and investment alternatives.

$ /yr
See the Results section for a full explanation of how this is used in your recommended income estimates.

The easiest way to calculate this: take your most recent pay stub and multiply your gross pay by the number of pay periods in a year (26 for bi-weekly, 24 for bi-monthly) to get your annual gross income. Multiply your total deducted taxes by the number of pay periods to get your annual tax deduction. Multiply your current monthly housing expenses by 12 to get your yearly housing costs. Then subtract your annual taxes and yearly housing costs from your annual gross pay. The result is your current annual lifestyle spending.

Note: If you're in the Renting & Saving scenario, you don't need to subtract your savings contributions from your lifestyle spending amount — the calculator accounts for those automatically.

Understanding Your Results

Your results are a starting point — not a verdict. And keep in mind that this is just the financial perspective; there may be things that matter more to you than a price tag.

Here's how to read your results:

If the buying column is mostly green and your PTR is blue or green, buying may be the stronger financial choice. This usually means renting is expensive enough relative to ownership that the savings advantage of renting is reduced or eliminated.

If the renting column is mostly green and your PTR is yellow, orange, or red, renting and saving the difference is probably the smarter move. Home prices in your scenario are high enough relative to rent that your savings and investments are likely to outgrow whatever you'd build in home equity — even accounting for appreciation.

A few things worth keeping in mind as you interpret your numbers: home value appreciation is never guaranteed. The profit you'd make selling your home depends heavily on market conditions, local demand, and timing — factors entirely outside your control. Long-term savings and investments, by contrast, are more flexible and have historically provided more reliable growth even through economic downturns.

And if renting makes more sense right now, that doesn't mean buying won't make sense later. Interest rates change, family situations evolve, and the math shifts. Saving and investing now is one of the best ways to keep your options open — including building a down payment if buying becomes the right move down the road.

This row shows everything you paid out of pocket over your chosen timeframe — every dollar that left your hands, on both sides of the comparison.

This is important because it answers the question: how much did your asset(s) actually cost you? Yes, you've built equity in your home or grown your savings — but what was the total cost to do so?

For buying, this includes your upfront costs (down payment, closing costs, moving, and reserves), plus all ongoing costs over the years: mortgage principal and interest, property taxes, insurance, HOA, maintenance, PMI, and utilities. The gain on sale is deliberately excluded here — it's included separately in the Financial Return row. This separation is intentional: it lets you see what you actually spent, independent of what you got back.

For renting, this includes your upfront costs (security deposit, moving, reserve, and initial deposit), plus all ongoing costs: rent, renters insurance, utilities, and your monthly savings contributions. Your savings contributions count as money spent here because they represent real dollars leaving your pocket each month — even though they're working for you in the background.

This row shows what you'd walk away with at the end of your chosen timeframe — the financial return on each path.

For buying, this is your home's projected value minus the remaining mortgage balance and sale closing costs. In other words, what you'd actually pocket if you sold the home at the end of the period. It accounts for appreciation over time but also for the significant cost of selling.

For renting, this is the total value of your savings and investments — your initial deposit plus all monthly contributions, compounded at your chosen rate of return over the full timeframe.

This is the bottom line. It takes your Financial Return and subtracts everything you spent to get there. A positive number means you came out ahead financially. A negative number means the costs outweighed the return over your chosen timeframe.

Green highlighting shows which option produces the better outcome on this row. But a word of caution: a higher number isn't always the right choice for your life. This is a financial comparison — it doesn't weigh stability, flexibility, community, or any of the other reasons people choose to buy or rent. Use it as one input in your decision, not the deciding factor.

The first recommended income row is based on the 30% rule — the common guidance from financial advisors that housing costs should represent no more than 30% of your income.

One important difference from the standard approach: while the typical 30% rule uses gross income, this calculator uses your net (take-home) income — the money you actually have available to spend after taxes. Using gross income for budgeting purposes is, frankly, lying to yourself about how much money you really have. Your purchasing decisions should be based on what you actually take home, not what you make on paper. Your effective income tax rate is then used to back-calculate the gross income equivalent.

It's also worth noting that this estimate assumes your lifestyle spending will scale with your new housing situation — that is, if you move into a more expensive home, your other spending will grow proportionally. This is sometimes called lifestyle inflation, and it's extremely common. If you don't expect your lifestyle spending to change much, the Current Lifestyle row gives you a more personalized estimate.

For the Renting column, this row assumes 30% for housing and 10% for savings — a total of 40% of net income — based on the savings parameters you provided.

This row takes a different approach. Instead of assuming your lifestyle will inflate to match your new income level, it assumes your current lifestyle spending stays roughly the same even after you move. That makes this a more personalized — and often more realistic — estimate for people who are disciplined about their spending.

To use this row effectively, enter your current yearly lifestyle spending in the Your Financial Picture section above.

One important caveat: it is extremely common for lifestyle spending to increase when someone moves into a new home or sees an increase in income. It takes real discipline to avoid lifestyle inflation. For that reason, treat this number as the minimum annual gross income required to afford the new home or rental — not a comfortable target.

For the Renting column, this row automatically accounts for your savings contributions when calculating the recommended gross income. You don't need to subtract savings from your lifestyle spending amount.
